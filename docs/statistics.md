# Numerical stability of statistical moments

The statistical and database functions share the helpers in `src/functions/statistical/moments.ts`. Means use compensated summation. Central sums use a corrected two-pass calculation, subtracting the correction for the rounded mean. This follows the corrected two-pass approach described by Chan, Golub and LeVeque (1983), also described in [Apache Commons Math's variance documentation](https://commons.apache.org/proper/commons-math/javadocs/api-3.6.1/org/apache/commons/math3/stat/descriptive/moment/Variance.html).

Univariate moments and paired regression share the `central` loop, including its correction and fallback decision. The degrees-of-freedom checks for variance and standard deviation live in `statistical/common.ts`, used by both statistical and database functions. Basic sum, sum of squares, product, minimum and maximum reductions live in `functions/common.ts`, preserving the original input order and each aggregate's empty-list result. Conditional math and statistical functions share range validation, SUMIF/AVERAGEIF resizing and criterion pairing in `functions/criteria.ts`.

`aggregateIf` and `aggregateIfs` also share the selection and error handling for SUMIF(S), AVERAGEIF(S), MAXIFS and MINIFS. The reduction is passed once per formula, keeping SUM's accumulation over selected positions separate from the statistical functions' collected numbers.

Ill-conditioned moments and intermediate overflow/underflow use `exact-moments.ts`: each finite input double is decoded as an integer times a power of two. BigInt accumulators keep the sums, squares and paired products exactly, with independent scales for x and y. The central sums, covariance and regression ratios are formed before conversion back to a double. The final division and square root still use double precision. No external numerical library is used.

The fallback also handles cancellation in a small intercept and in STEYX's residual variance. Large ordinary inputs continue through the double-precision path. Every input loop checks the calculation budget; calculation inside a function remains one synchronous evaluation step.

Excel's established domain behavior remains authoritative: the stored Excel answers for `STDEV.P(1E300,-1E300)` and its variants are `#NUM!`. An overflowing variance remains an error even when its mathematical square root would fit in a double.

`test/statistical-stability.test.ts` pins the large-mean/small-spread failure, small scales, constant large inputs, covariance across scales, small regression residuals and intercepts, database statistics and the recorded Excel overflow behavior. The large-offset example uses exact rational arithmetic on the stored binary values, rather than assuming decimal input values are represented exactly. A separate Fraction/Decimal check compared 60 generated datasets in both execution modes, 1,320 results, at relative tolerance 2e-12. These mathematical references supplement the saved Excel answers; the next section has Excel's answers on the same data.

The shared central loop was also compared with `9e6dd70`, using seven alternating measured pairs after two warmup pairs, ten calls per sample. At 34,000 ordinary values, variance took 0.168/0.135 ms and paired covariance 0.234/0.239 ms (before/after). At 10,000 values with a large offset and small spread, exercising the exact fallback, the corresponding times were 0.818/0.802 ms and 1.492/1.489 ms. The benchmark also checked exact equality of the returned doubles.

## Against Excel

The set `stat-edges-held` (`bench/coverage/sets/stat-edges-held.ts`, 111 cases) puts the data of the stability tests on a sheet and computes on them the variance, standard deviation, `DEVSQ`, `AVERAGE`, covariance, correlation, the regression functions, `LINEST` and the database statistics. The engine's answers were committed before Excel ran the set. Excel 16.113 for Mac and Excel 16.0.20430 for Windows gave the same answers, bit for bit, and 72 of them match the engine within the tests' tolerance (relative 1e-9, absolute below 1). The other 39 are known differences: Excel does not give the exact answer there and the engine does.

- Excel computes the moments in double precision about a mean it sums plainly (`momentRounding` in `test/excel-cases.ts`). Of 10,000 values 1E12 + 0, 0.01, 0.02 its mean is 1000000000000.0004 against 1000000000000.01, and the spread does not survive that: `VAR.P` 1.598E-04 against 6.680E-05, `DEVSQ` 1.598 against 0.668, `CORREL` with the steps 0, 1, 2 0.647 against 1, `STEYX` 0.0096 against 0, the database forms the same. `STEYX` of 1, 2.00000001, 3 on 1, 2, 3 (and of the intercept data) is 0 in Excel, 8.16E-09 exactly: the residual sum is lost to cancellation.
- Squares and products outside the range of doubles (`momentRange`): the squares of 1E-200 and 2E-200 are 0 in Excel, so `CORREL`, `PEARSON` and `RSQ` with 1, 2 and `SLOPE` of 1, 2 on them are `#DIV/0!`; three times 1E+308 overflows its sum, so `AVERAGE`, the variances, `DEVSQ`, `DAVERAGE` and `DVAR` are `#NUM!`; the covariance of 1E+308, −1E+308, 1E-200 with 0, 0, 1E+308 and the regression over them are `#NUM!` (2.2E+107 and 3.3E+107 for the covariances here).
- `LINEST` on the 10,000 values by their steps keeps the slope in both, Excel's 0.0100098 to 13.8 digits and the engine's the exact 0.010009765625: the engine takes y and the variables about their means before its Householder QR, which on the values as they are loses the digits of the large offset (0.0101547, the one prediction of the set Excel did not confirm where Excel is right).

Within the tolerance, so not pinned: the mean of the large values and what rests on it (`AVERAGE`, `INTERCEPT`, `FORECAST.LINEAR`, `DAVERAGE`, 14 digits; `COVARIANCE`, `SLOPE`, 13 to 14), the scale 1E150 (the 15th or 16th digit), `STDEV.P`, `STDEV.S`, `DSTDEV` and `DSTDEVP` of 1E-200 and 2E-200 (0 in Excel, 5E-201 and 7.07E-201 exactly: the absolute part of the tolerance), the small intercept after cancellation (`INTERCEPT` and `FORECAST` 3.3333333387E-09 or 3.3333331650E-09 in Excel against 3.3333333131E-09, 7 to 8 digits), `STEYX` at the strong correlation (11 digits), and `LINEST`'s standard error and intercept on the small data, where neither Excel nor the engine is exact (7 to 8 digits). The overflow of 1E+300 and −1E+300 is `#NUM!` in both, as before.

The set `fit-offset-held` (`bench/coverage/sets/fit-offset-held.ts`, 43 cases) computes `LINEST`, `LOGEST`, `TREND` and `GROWTH` on data far from 0 against their spread: the large-mean data with a second variable and without the constant, a line and a growth on x = 1E10 + 0…11, and two variables near 1E6 with y near 1E9. With a constant the engine takes y and each variable about their means before its Householder QR, and its coefficients and statistics there are within 1e-12 of exact least squares on the stored values. The engine's answers were committed before Excel ran the set. Both Excels gave the same answers, bit for bit, and 34 came out as predicted: every coefficient and standard error far from 0 is the engine's. Of the 9 misses, two are now the engine's rule: Excel evaluates `TREND` as b + m·x, which far from 0 keeps 8 digits of the fitted value (26.761363983 of the line at 1E10 + 12, exactly 26.761363636), and `GROWTH` here is evaluated the same way (313.839721 at 1E10 + 12, exactly 313.839727). The other 7 are known differences:

- Excel's intercept and regression sum of squares on the large-mean data follow the mean it sums plainly (`linestMean`): the intercept is 1E12 − 0.0096 against 1E12, the regression sum of squares 1.598, Excel's `DEVSQ`, against 0.668, and `TREND` at step 3 follows the intercept, 1E12 + 0.0204 against 1E12 + 0.0300.
- `GROWTH` over x = 1E10 + k is `#NUM!` in Excel (`growthRange`), where the fitted values lie between 285 and 314.
